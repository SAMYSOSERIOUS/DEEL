Did Germany really save electricity in the 2022 energy crisis?

Generated 2026-10-10T11:29:36+00:00 from data up to 2026-10-10 09:00:00+00:00.

The short answer

Germany used 8.8% less grid electricity in winter 2022/23 than the weather predicted (90% range 6.2% to 11.2%).

In winter 2022/23 (October to March), Germany's grid delivered 8.8% less electricity than the weather and calendar predicted (90% range 6.2% to 11.2%), about 23.1 TWh. Demand has NOT fully come back: in 2025/26 it was still 4.2% below the pre-crisis pattern (range 1.5% to 6.8%). Counting only dark hours (when rooftop solar can't hide demand), the 2022/23 saving was 9.2%.

When did the saving happen, and who might have saved?

In winter 2022/23 the saving was 8.8% on workdays and 8.8% on weekends and holidays (8.4% in workday daytime, 7:00-18:00).

Energy-hungry industries (Paper, Chemicals, Glass, cement, ceramics, Basic metals) produced -12.6% compared with 2021 during winter 2022/23. Month by month, the saving and the drop in industrial output move together with a correlation of -0.40 (1 = in perfect step, 0 = unrelated) over 48 months.

What this analysis can and can't tell apart

  1. Grid demand is not total use. SMARD measures electricity flowing through the public grid. Power that rooftop solar panels make and use on the spot never shows up. Rooftop solar grew fast after 2022, so part of the "saving" in daytime hours may just be self-made solar power. The dark hours only view removes this: at night no panel produces anything.
  2. Industry or households? One national number per hour can't say who saved. Two hints only: when the saving happened (workdays and daytime point to industry and offices; evenings and weekends point to homes), and whether energy-hungry industries produced less at the same time (Eurostat). A match in timing is a hint, not proof.
  3. Saving or cutting back? A factory that stops making ammonia "saves" electricity, but that is lost production, not efficiency. The data can't tell a smarter fridge from a closed plant.
  4. Prices or appeals? Prices jumped and the government asked people to save at the same time. Both pushed the same way in the same months, so their effects can't be separated here.
  5. New demand hides savings. Heat pumps and electric cars added demand after 2022. The estimate is the net change: real savings could be larger than shown.
  6. The further ahead, the less sure. The "no crisis" model learned from 2015 to August 2022. Every winter after that is forecast further into the future, so the ranges get wider. The model holds the last pre-crisis year's level; it can't know about slow changes after that.
  7. Starting date matters a little. Prices already rose from autumn 2021. Training up to September 2022 may already include some early crisis behaviour, which would make the saving look smaller. The cutoff views show how much this changes the answer.
